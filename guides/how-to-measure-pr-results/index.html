How to measure PR results beyond counting coverage

Guide & reviewed by Rizky Wicaksono · Drafted with AI assistance · 25 November 2026 · 5 min read

In short

Count coverage, but judge it. Track the quality of outlets, whether your key messages appear, the traffic and actions that follow, and tie everything to a business goal. Avoid advertising value equivalency (AVE).

Start with the objective

Measurement starts before the campaign. Decide what the PR is meant to change: awareness, reputation, sign-ups, attendance, sales leads. Without an objective, every number looks impressive and none of them means anything.

Separate outputs from outcomes

Outputs are what you produce: press releases, interviews, articles published. Outcomes are what changes because of them: people visiting, registering, buying or changing their opinion. Report both, and put more weight on outcomes.

Metrics worth tracking

  • Coverage volume and quality: how many articles, in which outlets, and how relevant those outlets are to your audience. Grouping outlets into tiers, for example tier 1, 2 and 3, can make this clear.
  • Message pull-through: how often your key messages appear in the coverage.
  • Share of voice: your visibility compared with competitors on the same topic.
  • Referral traffic and actions: visits from articles, plus sign-ups, enquiries or sales that follow.
  • Sentiment: whether the tone toward you is positive, neutral or negative.
  • Spokesperson visibility: interviews and quotes secured.

Why to avoid AVE

Advertising value equivalency (AVE) puts a price on coverage by comparing it with what the same space would cost as advertising. AMEC, the industry body that maintains the Barcelona Principles (now in version 4.0), runs a “Say No to AVEs” initiative that asks organisations to stop using AVE and its derivatives as metrics. It also publishes a guide, “22 Reasons Not to Use AVEs”, which is available in Indonesian. If a client still asks for AVE, explain why it misleads and offer the metrics above instead.

An example of pairing coverage with a result

A coverage count is more convincing next to a business outcome. In the JAFF Market campaign, the 663 media coverages came alongside 1,120 approved business meetings on the platform. Together they tell a story: attention was generated, and it turned into participation.

Report it simply

Keep the monthly report short: the objective, the three or four most important numbers, the best pieces of coverage, what you learned and what you will change next month. Decision-makers read two clear pages; they skip twenty.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good number of media coverages?

There is no universal number. It depends on your objective, your market and your budget. Compare against your own earlier campaigns and against what matters to your audience.

What is the difference between reach and impressions?

Reach counts unique people who could have seen something. Impressions count every view, including repeats by the same person. Always say which one you report.

How often should I report?

Monthly for ongoing work, and a summary after each campaign or launch.
