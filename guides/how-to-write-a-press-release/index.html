How to write a press release journalists will actually read

Draft by AI · Reviewed by Rizky Wicaksono · 25 November 2026 · 6 min read

In short

A good press release is short and built around one piece of news. Answer who, what, when, where and why in the first paragraph, add one or two useful quotes, attach ready-to-use assets, and give a contact who will actually pick up. Never offer money or gifts to influence coverage.

Start with the news, not the company

Before writing, ask two questions: why is this news, and why now? A new product, a result, a partnership, an event or a change of leadership can be news. “We are proud of our company” is not. If there is no news, a press release is the wrong tool; a feature pitch or a blog post usually fits better.

Write a specific headline

Say what happened in plain words, in one short line. Include the concrete fact: the number, the place, the name. Skip hype words such as “revolutionary” or “world-class”. A journalist should understand the story from the headline alone.

Put the story in the first paragraph

The first paragraph, short and self-contained, should answer who, what, when, where and why. Journalists often decide whether to keep reading from this paragraph, so everything essential belongs here. This is the classic inverted pyramid: the most important facts first, context after.

Keep the body short and sourced

  • Use short paragraphs of two or three sentences.
  • Add one or two data points, each with its source.
  • Put background and context after the facts, not before.
  • Aim for one page.

Use a quote that adds something

A quote should say something a person would say out loud: an opinion, a reason, a bit of insight. It should not repeat the facts already in the text. Name the speaker and their role, and get their approval before sending. Many PR writing guides suggest two quotes: one from your organisation and one from a third party, such as a partner or customer.

Finish with a boilerplate and a real contact

Add two or three sentences describing the organisation, then the name, email and phone number of the contact person (in Indonesian releases, the narahubung). Choose someone who will respond quickly. A release with no reachable contact loses stories, because journalists work against deadlines.

Make it easy to use

Journalists need assets they can publish. Share high-resolution photos with captions and credits, logos, a short fact sheet and the availability of a spokesperson. Link to a shared folder instead of attaching heavy files.

  • Language: write in the language the outlet publishes in (for national Indonesian media, usually Bahasa Indonesia), and prepare an English version if you also target international outlets.
  • Time zones: for events, state the time with WIB, WITA or WIT so nobody has to guess.

Keep your media relations clean

Indonesian journalists are bound by the Journalistic Code of Ethics (Kode Etik Jurnalistik). The Press Law (UU No. 40/1999, Article 7 paragraph 2) says journalists must have and follow it. Article 6 of the code says journalists must not abuse their profession or accept bribes, and its interpretation is broad: a bribe includes gifts of money, goods or facilities from another party that affect independence (as reproduced in a secondary copy of the code; check the official text).

For PR, that means: never offer money or gifts to influence coverage. Keep hospitality at events modest and transparent, and follow both your client’s rules and the outlet’s own policies. Media organisations and journalist associations have their own codes, so ask the outlet what it allows. Paid content is a separate arrangement. The Dewan Pers’s guideline for online media (Pedoman Pemberitaan Media Siber, 2012) requires media to keep news and advertising separate and to label paid content, for example as “advertorial”, “iklan” or “sponsored”, so never present paid placement as editorial coverage.

Checklist before you send

  • Is there one clear piece of news?
  • Does the headline state the fact?
  • Does the first paragraph answer who, what, when, where and why?
  • Is every number checked and sourced?
  • Is the quote approved and worth reading?
  • Are the contact details correct, and will that person answer?
  • Are the photos and assets ready to download?

Frequently asked questions

How long should a press release be?

Ideally one page. If you need more, put the extra detail in a fact sheet or a shared folder.

Should I send a press release as an attachment?

Paste it into the body of the email so it can be read at once. You can add a PDF or document as a convenience, but never as the only copy.

Do I need to pay media to publish my press release?

Editorial coverage should not be bought. If you want guaranteed placement, that is advertising or sponsored content, and it should be labelled as paid.

Do I need a press release for every announcement?

No. Use one when there is real news. For softer stories, a short pitch to a specific journalist often works better.
