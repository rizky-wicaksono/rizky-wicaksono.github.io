<h2>Minutes record decisions, not conversation</h2>
<p>A transcript tells you everything that was said. Minutes tell the reader what changed because of the meeting. Most people who read your minutes were not in the room, or were in the room and remember it differently. They want four things:</p><ul><li><strong>Agenda:</strong> what the meeting was for.</li><li><strong>Decisions:</strong> what was agreed, in one plain sentence each.</li><li><strong>Open points:</strong> what was discussed but not decided, and who will settle it.</li><li><strong>Action items:</strong> who does what, and by when. A task without an owner and a date is a wish.</li></ul><p>Add the basics at the top (title, date, place or platform, participants, who prepared it) and you have minutes. Everything else is optional.</p>

<h2>Minutes of meeting or contact report?</h2>
<p>In PR agencies the same notes often go out as a <strong>contact report</strong>: a short record sent to the client after a meeting or call, listing what was agreed and who does what. It is a working document, not a formality. If a client later asks why the press release went out on Tuesday, the contact report that said “client approved Tuesday release, 10:00 WIB” is your answer.</p><p>That is why many agencies end a contact report with a line such as: “If any of the above differs from your understanding, please let us know within two working days.” It turns silence into agreement, and it gives the client a fair chance to correct you.</p>

<h2>Who owns the minutes?</h2>
<p>Whoever writes the minutes writes the record. That is a quiet kind of power. Three weeks later, nobody replays the recording; they open the minutes. A decision that is phrased softly (“the client is open to a Tuesday release”) can be read later as approval or as nothing at all.</p><p>Some questions worth asking before you hit send (practitioner advice, not a rule):</p><ul><li><strong>Would the other side sign this?</strong> Minutes that quietly favour the writer damage trust once someone notices. Write decisions so both sides would recognise them.</li><li><strong>Is it a decision or a hope?</strong> “We will try to get coverage in Kompas” is not a commitment to Kompas coverage. Keep the client’s expectations and the minutes the same.</li><li><strong>Who will read it?</strong> Minutes get forwarded. If something sensitive was said about a journalist, a competitor or a staff member, ask whether it needs to be in writing at all.</li></ul>

<h2>Why you still need the recording</h2>
<p>Automatic transcripts are good, and they are still wrong in predictable places: names, numbers, brand names, mixed Indonesian and English, and short words. The short words are the dangerous ones. “Kami <em>belum</em> sepakat” and “kami sepakat” are one word apart, and so are “it’s <em>not</em> approved” and “it’s approved”. A transcript that drops that word turns an open point into a decision.</p><p>The Meeting Minutes Maker flags phrases like “belum sepakat” or “not decided yet” as open points, but it cannot hear the recording. Before you send minutes, listen again to every decision, every deadline and every number. Each suggestion keeps its timestamp, so you can jump straight to that moment in the recording.</p>

<h2>Client meetings are confidential, so is the transcript</h2>
<p>A client meeting transcript can hold unreleased results, crisis plans, embargoed news and personal opinions about named people. Pasting it into a free online summariser sends all of that to someone else’s server, often under terms nobody in the meeting read. Under Indonesia’s Personal Data Protection Law (UU PDP), voices and names in a recording can be personal data too.</p><ul><li>Tell people the meeting is recorded, and why.</li><li>Agree with the client where recordings and transcripts are kept, and for how long.</li><li>Prefer tools that work on your own device. The Meeting Minutes Maker runs entirely in your browser, makes no network requests while you work, and saves nothing unless you ask it to.</li></ul><p>Recorded an interview rather than a meeting? See <a href="../how-to-make-an-audio-file-smaller/index.html">how to make an audio file smaller</a> before you send it on.</p>

<h2>How PR teams use this</h2>
<ul><li><strong>Client status meetings:</strong> a contact report the same day, with action items split between agency and client.</li><li><strong>Media interview prep:</strong> a record of the agreed key messages, the no-go topics and who briefs the spokesperson.</li><li><strong>Event and press conference planning:</strong> decisions on date, venue, guest list and run-down, with owners and deadlines.</li><li><strong>Crisis calls:</strong> a timestamped record of who approved which statement, and when.</li></ul>
