<h2>What GEO means, and what Google says</h2>
<p>Generative engine optimization (GEO) is a term for making your brand and content easier for AI tools to find, understand and cite. A 2023 research paper by Aggarwal and colleagues (published at KDD 2024) gave it its name and tested content changes such as adding sources, quotations and statistics. It reported visibility gains of up to 40% on its own benchmark, little benefit from keyword stuffing, and results that varied by topic.</p><p>Google’s guide on generative AI features says you do not need new machine-readable files, AI text files, markup or special formats to appear in Google Search, and that optimizing for generative AI search is still SEO. It also tells readers to check third-party “GEO” or “AEO” advice against Google’s own guidance. This is Google’s position for Google Search; ChatGPT, Perplexity and others publish their own, different documentation. Google Search ignores llms.txt files, and no official statement from the other companies says otherwise.</p>

<h2>Be indexable and eligible for a snippet</h2>
<p>According to Google, to be eligible for its generative AI features a page must be indexed and eligible to appear in Search with a snippet. Indexing is not guaranteed. In practice: let search engines crawl your pages, give each page a clear title and description, list your pages in a sitemap, and use Search Console to see what is indexed. Google’s preview controls such as nosnippet and noindex limit how your content appears, including in AI features, so use them on purpose.</p><p>Other AI search products have their own crawlers. OpenAI documents OAI-SearchBot, which surfaces sites in ChatGPT search, and Perplexity documents PerplexityBot; both say you should allow them in robots.txt if you want to appear. Blocking a training crawler such as GPTBot is a separate choice.</p>

<h2>Publish helpful, people-first content</h2>
<p>Google’s guidance on helpful content asks whether your content gives original information or analysis, goes into real depth, shows first-hand experience, cites its sources and is clear about who wrote it and why. It encourages accurate bylines and says it helps readers to know how content was produced, including when AI assisted, where readers would reasonably expect that. Google’s May 2025 blog post on its AI experiences points the same way: unique, non-commodity content that satisfies visitors.</p>

<h2>Avoid scaled content</h2>
<p>Google warns that producing content at scale primarily to manipulate rankings or AI responses violates its scaled content abuse spam policy. For a person or a small brand, the lesson is simple: fewer, better pages beat many thin ones.</p>

<h2>Earn credible mentions elsewhere (where PR helps)</h2>
<p>Some evidence suggests AI search tools lean on third-party, authoritative sources. A 2025 preprint from the University of Toronto found that the AI search engines it tested cited earned media far more than brand-owned or social content, unlike Google’s more balanced mix, in the consumer-product categories it studied. Muck Rack, a PR software vendor, publishes reports on what AI tools cite and reports mostly non-paid sources, but its figures differ by edition and its method is not fully shown.</p><p>Treat this as directional, not a rule: the evidence is early, covers specific categories and engines, and part of it is vendor research. The practical takeaway matches classic PR work: relevant stories, good relationships with journalists, and clear facts they can quote. The GEO paper above tested on-page changes, not earned media, so it does not show that earned media drives citation.</p>

<h2>Keep your facts consistent (practitioner advice)</h2>
<p>Use the same name spelling, job title, description and key facts across your website, LinkedIn, press releases and boilerplates. This is a practitioner’s recommendation, not a rule from Google or OpenAI, but Microsoft’s Bing guidance also recommends consistent details about the same entities. When sources agree, it is easier for both readers and machines to be sure who you are and what you do.</p>

<h2>Check what AI tools say about you (practitioner advice)</h2>
<p>Every month or two, ask a few AI tools about your brand or your name and note what they say and which sources they cite. No one has tested a best frequency, so adjust to your needs. If something is wrong, find the source it came from and fix or update that page. Bing’s Webmaster Tools now include an AI Performance report (public preview, February 2026) that shows which of your pages are cited in Copilot and Bing AI summaries; it does not show ranking or position.</p>
